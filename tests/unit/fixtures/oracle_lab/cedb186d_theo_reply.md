Your idea mostly holds up. The management literature supports the core claim, but it disqualifies a strict tree of goals that cascades mechanically from the top. The version that works is shared intent and priorities at every level, agreed in both directions, and kept deliberately shallow.

**What supports it**
- **Autonomy needs alignment first.** Netflix calls it "highly aligned, loosely coupled". Spotify's model calls it "aligned autonomy". People can only choose well themselves when they know what matters and why.
- **Military "commander's intent" is your idea almost exactly.** Stephen Bungay's book *The Art of Action* says each level passes down the purpose one level up. People then decide the how themselves. His finding is that more detailed plans make teams slower, while clearer intent makes them faster.
- **Clear goals measurably help.** Goal-setting research by Locke and Latham, and Google's study of effective teams, both find clarity about what matters drives performance.

**Where the pure version breaks**
- **Strict cascading goes stale and becomes bureaucracy.** Christina Wodtke and others found that mechanically splitting objectives level by level creates overhead. The tree is out of date within weeks.
- **Pure top-down loses information.** Hoshin Kanri, the Japanese strategy method, uses "catchball". Goals are proposed downward and pushed back upward. The people nearest the work know what is actually possible.
- **"Increase company value" is too abstract to prioritise with.** You're right to call it an axiom rather than a goal. Richard Rumelt's point is that a goal isn't a strategy. You need a diagnosis of the challenge and a guiding policy.
- **Goals alone don't settle trade-offs.** Two tasks can both serve a goal. The team still needs an explicit ranking, like "margin over new features this quarter".

**What it would look like at Example Co**
1. **The axiom** is to grow company value, which the owner will eventually realise.
2. **The diagnosis** from your notes is that revenue grew only about 3 percent while acquisitions drive growth. So organic growth and margin from integration are what the owner cares about.
3. **Company priorities** follow from that. Examples are one shared service catalogue, cheaper delivery, and cross-selling between the acquired companies.
4. **Team bets per iteration** are the one-epic-per-person outcomes from yesterday's board proposal. Each should name which priority it serves.
5. **Tasks** are then prioritised by each person against the bet, without asking the lead.

Three or four levels is enough. Each level only needs to know the level above and the reason for it.

**Why this helps your overwhelmed team lead**
- Right now he is the prioritisation bottleneck, because only he holds the context.
- Written intent and priorities move that context out of his head. The team can decide most things alone and escalate only real conflicts.
- Proposing and maintaining that structure is a natural management role for you. It also fits the career path in your notes.

**Open questions**
- Who owns the company-level priorities: the CTO, the CTO office, or someone higher?
- How often is the structure revisited? Quarterly for priorities and every two weeks for bets is a common rhythm.
- How does the team settle a conflict between two priorities without going back to the lead?
